The problem

Every wallet does what you say — even when it costs you everything.

Wallets optimize for executing your instruction. They confirm; they don't refuse. So the failure mode is silent: a lookalike address, one mistyped hex character, a max-value transfer you didn't mean, a route that leaves your funds somewhere they can't come back from.

How it works

AI proposes. Deterministic code verifies. The device signature disposes.

Natural language becomes a typed intent through a deterministic parser — free, instant, no model round-trip for common asks (the LLM is only a fallback). The planner then verifies it against the real world before anything is signable.

plain English→ deterministic parser→ typed Intent→ planner + guards→ device signature
  • Multilingual by design — English, Hinglish, Korean parse to the same intent. 217 parser tests, zero “couldn't understand that” on the corpus.
  • The plan is a receipt — balance, recipient validity, chain, route, risk and cost are all shown and checked before a signature is possible.
  • Nothing is simulated — the device signature disposes, or nothing does. No “confirmed” that didn't happen on-chain.
Sentinel · the security layer

Five checks between your words and your keys.

One rule, five expressions of it: when the wallet can't safely deliver what you asked, it refuses to sign — and says why.

01

Address poisoning

Catches a lookalike on a first-ever send, with no saved contact — on-chain history tells the fake from the real.

02

EIP-55 checksum

One mistyped character in an address fails its checksum and is stopped before it can burn your funds.

03

Cumulative drain guard

A drain split across several under-threshold sends is caught on the one that completes it — proven on the deployed path by fix-revert.

04

Recipient never dropped

If a phrasing isn't fully understood, an address is never silently discarded — the wallet asks instead of guessing.

05

Undeliverable-route refusal

A route the wallet can't finish end-to-end is refused before signing — not attempted and lost.

rule

Refuse, don't guess.

Restraint as a feature — the thing that makes the other four one idea.

Cross-chain, honestly

A canonical bridge — no third-party bridge risk on our home route.

Bridges are crypto's most-exploited surface. So the wallet's cross-chain route uses GIWA's canonical OP Stack bridge — the chain's own infrastructure, non-custodial, ~60 seconds, both legs proven above.

  • Ethereum Sepolia → GIWA — canonical L1StandardBridge. Scales to any number of users with zero custody, because it's the chain's own infra, not a service we run.
  • Same-chain swaps — ETH ⇄ gUSDC on GIWA's SimpleAMM, SOL ⇄ dUSDC on Solana's solAMM, signed in your browser.
  • Broader cross-chain — routed through audited aggregators on mainnet, behind our RouteProvider seam. We ship what's proven and label the rest roadmap — never “trustless” when it isn't.
The discipline is the product. A wallet that knows the difference between what it can execute and what it can only promise — and refuses to sign the second kind.

Architecture

A seam, not a rewrite.

A 6-layer strict-DAG monorepo — chains build, core signs — with float-free money paths and 600+ tests. Routing is a plug: the same interface that composes GIWA's AMM, Jupiter and Uniswap today accepts a mainnet aggregator tomorrow, and the safety layer doesn't change.

600+

tests across parser, planner, guards, chains and the deployed API path.

Non-custodial

keys are generated and sealed on device — they never touch a server.

Float-free

every money path is exact integer math — no floating-point drift, ever.

“How does it scale, who holds the funds?” The canonical bridge needs no operator and no float — infinite users, zero custody. Aggregators are non-custodial by construction. We never hold funds in transit.
